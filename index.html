About Me

I received my Bachelor of Science in physics from East Texas A&M University (ETAMU), and I am currently a second-year PhD astrophysics student at Southern Methodist University (SMU). I am working toward a career in academia because I am passionate about learning, teaching, and researching our place in the Universe and how it works. To contribute to our understanding of how the Universe works, I want to work on exoplanet detection, follow-up, characterization, modeling, and simulation. To help humanity further constrain our place/role in the Universe, I would love to work on astrobiology and the Search for Extraterrestrial Intelligence (SETI). I believe that one of the biggest discoveries we ever make will be of the existence of life having developed elsewhere. Whether the life we discover is intelligent, microbial, hippo-like, or somewhere in between, I believe its existence will have profound effects on our society as a whole.

I discovered my love for education as an undergraduate, when I tutored for various math, physics, astronomy, engineering, chemistry, and computer science courses. After enjoying tutoring so much, I decided I should also be an undergraduate Learning Assistant (LA), where I would help students work through in-class physics/astronomy tutorials and problem sets. I also gained experience in teaching younger students (K-12) physics concepts from my time as an officer for the Society of Physics Students at ETAMU. I performed physics demos at several K-12 outreach events in the hopes of igniting and encouraging a love for learning about the Universe. Teaching has overall made me a better student and researcher because it has given me practice in articulating and challenging my understanding of a given topic.

Research

During my undergraduate career at ETAMU, I conducted observational astronomy research with Professor Kurtis Williams on searching for rapidly rotating massive white dwarfs (WDs). Just like the Sun, a WD can have star spots that change its measured luminosity as it rotates. We can measure this variation in brightness and use it to determine the star's rotational period. The ultimate goal of my advisor was to use what I might find to contribute to constraining the fraction of rapidly rotating massive WD stars that result from a double degenerate WD star merger. We defined a rapidly rotating massive WD star to have a period less than or equal to 6 hours and a mass greater than or equal to 0.9 solar masses. For this work, I used the ETAMU Observatory telescopes to observe several targets over many nights. We found that the data strongly suggested one of our targets, LAMOST J132743.78+545725.7, was a rapidly rotating massive WD.

As a graduate student at SMU, I am currently working on several astronomy and astrophysics projects with Professor Robert Kehoe. I have worked on modeling interacting binary star systems (ROTSE telescope archival data and PHOEBE) along with modeling Delta Scuti variable stars (MESA). I have acquired hands-on telescope hardware and software experience by aiding in the recommissioning of the ROTSE-IIIb telescope. I have also been assisting in developing and debugging the ROTSE binary star data analysis pipeline. We have recently begun a small TOI follow-up exoplanet project using ROTSE archival data. For this project, we hope to contribute to the analysis and characterization of the systems we have identified in our data, while also exploring the limits of ROTSE's capabilities.

To get a better idea of my research experience, you can take a look at the slides and video that are linked in the Presentations section of this website. You can also see how I have been working to strengthen my abilities as a researcher in the Professional Development section.
